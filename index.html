1Why contract workThe odds and why they favor you

A contract job is a real job at a real company. The difference is who signs your paycheck. You work on site or remote for the client, say an aerospace plant or a distribution center, but you're on the payroll of a staffing agency the client pays to find people.

Big companies do this all the time. When a team needs help fast, finance approves a contract seat long before it approves a permanent one. The agency gets the job order and has days, not months, to fill it. That urgency is your advantage.

400–700applications in one day on a company's own permanent posting
2–4applications in a week on an agency's contract posting

Numbers from real job searches, not a study. Your field and city will differ. The gap is the point.

The bottom lineMost people ignore recruiter messages because they're scared of scams, or they bail after one call with an overseas recruiter. Answer the phone, send your resume fast, and follow through, and you're ahead of almost everyone in line.
2Know before you applyFour things that trip up almost everyone
Your recruiter may be overseas. That's normal.

Many US staffing firms run recruiting teams offshore to cut costs. Don't hang up on a real opportunity because of an accent or a time zone. Judge the job and the company, not the voice.

Verify the recruiter before you hand over anything.

A real recruiter writes from the agency's own domain, like name@roberthalf.com, not Gmail or Outlook. Look them up on LinkedIn and on the agency site. The Warnings tab has the full checklist.

The recruiter needs you as much as you need them.

Recruiters get paid when you get placed. You don't have to impress them. Be polite, answer fast, and be straight about what you want and what you'll accept.

Send your resume fast. Minutes matter.

The client gives the agency a few interview slots, and several agencies chase the same job. Once the slots fill, nobody else gets submitted. Keep a current Word and PDF copy on your phone so you can send it within the hour.

3Cons and prosThe honest trade-offs, side by side
No PTO or sick leaveTake a day off and you don't get paid for it. Plan appointments around your schedule or ask for a shift swap.
Higher hourly payContract rates usually run above the same job on staff, partly to cover the benefits you're not getting. Some agencies also offer health and 401(k) plans to W-2 contractors. Ask.
No guarantee of an extensionSix months may really mean six months. Keep your resume current and stay in touch with your recruiter the whole time.
A shot at a Fortune 500 companyA big name on your resume opens doors you couldn't get through before. One good contract changes how every future recruiter sees you.
Thin benefits, or noneIf you don't have VA health care, look into enrolling before you start. It covers the biggest gap contract work leaves.
You can test-drive the companyWorking a contract at a place like RTX, Boeing or NASA tells you what it's really like. Leaving at the end of a six-month contract looks far better on a resume than quitting a perm job at month six.
No paid holidaysOff on a holiday? No pay that day. Working one? Usually regular pay, no premium.
More schedule flexibilityYou can often ask for a schedule change from day one if the team's workload allows. Tell your recruiter and your onsite lead. Best for one-offs, like shifting hours around a VA appointment.
You have to give notice to leaveMost agencies want notice before you quit. Find out how much before you sign. Skip it and the agency can contest your unemployment or mark you not eligible for rehire, and some contracts carry a fee. Run the agreement through an AI tool before signing so you catch what tired eyes miss.
A recruiter in your corner for yearsFinish a contract in good standing and that agency knows you. Five years from now, if a job goes bad, you call them. They move faster for people they've already placed.
You won't hear the company's plans until lateExpect to learn about a perm offer or an extension only 1 to 2 months before the contract ends. Budgets run by quarter. The team asks, finance approves. Your boss can't control that timeline no matter how much they like you.
Much better odds of getting hiredContract postings draw a fraction of the applicants a company's own ads do, and many of those drop out after the first recruiter call. You won't.
4Learn the lingoSound like you've done this before
W-2 contract
You're an employee of the agency. Taxes withheld, employer pays its share of Social Security, and you can usually draw unemployment between contracts. This is what you want.
1099
Self-employed. No taxes withheld, and you pay both halves of Social Security (about 15.3%). Only take 1099 if the rate covers that gap.
C2C
Corp-to-corp. The agency pays your own company, not you. Only makes sense if you already run an LLC. Most veterans starting out should skip these.
RTR
Right to represent. Says this one agency can submit you for this one job. Never sign two RTRs for the same job. Two agencies submitting you looks bad and the client may toss you out.
Req number
The job order number. Write it down. It's how you track which agency has you on which job.
Contract-to-hire
You start as a contractor with the goal of a permanent hire, often after 3 to 6 months. A goal, not a promise.
Pay vs. bill rate
Pay rate is what you get. Bill rate is what the client pays the agency. The gap is the agency's margin. You only negotiate pay rate.
MSP / VMS
Managed service provider / vendor management system. Large companies route contract hiring through one system and only approved agencies can submit. That's why some jobs are only reachable through an agency.
Non-exempt
Hourly and eligible for overtime. Most contract roles are non-exempt, so ask what the overtime rate is.
ATS
Applicant tracking system. The software that reads your resume before a human does. Use the exact words from the posting (job title, systems, certs) so it scores you high.
Submittal
When the agency sends your resume to the client. Most clients block a second submittal of the same person for 6 to 12 months, so make sure the first one is a good fit.
Conversion fee
What the client pays the agency to hire you permanently. One reason a perm offer can take a while. Not your cost.

Section 2

The Playbook

Step by step, from first click to first paycheck. Work several agencies at once. Keep each job with one agency.

1The eight stepsDo them in this order
  1. Get your resume mission-ready.Two pages max. Put the civilian job title you want right under your name. Civilian words, not military ones: "managed $1.2M in parts orders," not "S-4 supply NCOIC." Numbers on every bullet: dollars, headcount, aircraft, pallets. Mirror the words in the posting so the ATS scores you. Save a Word copy and a PDF on your phone. Free help: Hire Heroes USA will rewrite it with you (Gems tab).
  2. Find the posting on the agency's board.Search the agency sites in the Agencies tab for your job title plus "contract." Also search LinkedIn and Indeed, then confirm the poster is an agency. Apply within 48 hours of a posting going up. Anything older than two weeks is probably filled.
  3. Apply, then call.Apply online first so you're in their system. Then call the recruiter or the branch the same day. Use the phone script below. Voicemail? Leave your name, the job title, the req number and that your resume is in their inbox. Then email it with the subject line "Req # · Job title · Your name."
  4. Sign the RTR for that one job.Read it. It should name the client or job and the pay rate. One agency per job, period.
  5. Interview with the client.Usually a 30-minute phone or video call with the hiring manager. For contract roles they want two things: can you start soon, and can you do the job without hand-holding. Have three short stories ready that prove it. Ask your recruiter what the manager cares about most. They often know. Close with "What does the plan look like after the contract term?"
  6. Get the offer in writing.Pay rate, overtime rate, start date, length, location and schedule. Ask the questions below before you say yes.
  7. Knock out onboarding fast.Background check, drug screen, I-9 and direct deposit. Drug screen orders expire, often within days, so go right away. Have ready: a passport, or a driver's license plus your Social Security card or birth certificate (for the I-9), a voided check for direct deposit, and your DD-214 Member 4 copy.
  8. Show up and own it.Turn in your timecard on time every week. Late timecards mean late pay. Around month 3 or 4, ask your recruiter, not just your manager, what the plan is for extension or conversion.
Best method for successApply to the posting, then call the recruiter that same day and tell them you want that contract. An application sitting in a system is easy to miss. A veteran on the phone saying "I'm ready, here's my resume, I can interview tomorrow" is not.
$Negotiating your rateMost people leave money on the table here
  • Ask for the range before you give a number. "What's the pay range on this req?" Every agency has a spread between the floor and the max the client's bill rate allows.
  • Then ask near the top of it. Asking for $2 to $4 an hour more than the first number is normal and expected. Recruiters rarely pull an offer over that.
  • Never name a number first if you can help it. If pushed, give a range with your real target at the bottom.
  • Get the overtime rate in writing. Non-exempt contract roles pay time and a half. Ask whether OT is approved on this job.
  • Ask when you're paid. Weekly is common and helps you between paychecks. Ask about first-check timing, which can be two to three weeks out.
  • Don't invent other offers. Recruiters talk to each other and to the client. A real competing rate is leverage. A fake one ends the conversation.
↻Follow-up rhythmPersistent, not pushy
  • Same day: apply, call, email the resume.
  • 3 business days later: one short email or call. "Checking whether I was submitted and if the client needs anything else from me."
  • Weekly after that until you hear yes or no. One touch a week. Not daily.
  • Every call ends with: "What else do you have that isn't posted?"
  • After a no: thank them and ask to be kept in mind. That recruiter is now a contact, not a dead end.
  • Answer unknown numbers during your search. Set a short, professional voicemail greeting with your name.
3The phone scriptUnder a minute. Say it, don't read it.

"Hi, this is [your name]. I just applied for the [job title] contract you posted, req number [#] if you have it.

I'm a [branch] veteran with [X] years in [your field], and I'm available to start [date]. I've got my resume ready to send right now.

Who's the best person to talk to about getting submitted?"

Then send the resume while you're still on the phone, or the minute you hang up.

Working more than one agency
  • Work three to five at the same time. Each has different clients. More agencies, more doors.
  • One agency per job. If two recruiters pitch the same role, go with the one who called first and tell the other you're already submitted.
  • Keep a short list of recruiters who deliver. After a few weeks you'll know who calls back.
  • Tell them you're a veteran. Several of these firms track veteran hires and some have military recruiting teams.
Section 4

Warnings

Fake recruiters are the reason real ones get ignored. Scammers want one of three things: your personal data, your money, or your bank account. Real agencies never need any of those before a written offer.

!Red flags: walk awayAny one of these is enough
  • Gmail, Outlook or Yahoo address for a "national agency"
  • Interview only by text, Telegram, WhatsApp or Signal
  • Asks for your SSN, bank info or a copy of your ID before an offer
  • Sends you a check to buy equipment, then asks you to send some back
  • Part-time pay that's way too high for the job
  • Wants you to email your info to an address off the job site
  • Pressures you to decide in minutes or pay any kind of fee
  • A job offer with no interview at all
  • Asks you to pay for training, a certification or equipment to start
✓Green flags: likely realWhat a legit recruiter looks like
  • Email comes from the agency's own domain
  • Recruiter has a real LinkedIn profile at that agency, with connections and history
  • The job names a real client or gives a real location and req number
  • They want a phone or video call before anything else
  • Background check and drug screen come after a written offer, through a known vendor like Quest
  • Pay is W-2 through a company you can look up and call
60The 60-second checkDo this before you reply to anyone
  1. Search the recruiter's name plus the agency on LinkedIn.
  2. Go to the agency's website yourself (don't click their link) and find the job on their board.
  3. Call the agency's main number from their website and ask if the recruiter works there.
  4. Still not sure? Paste the message into an AI tool and ask if it looks like a scam.
§Contract trapsRead before you sign
  • Two RTRs on one job. The client can throw out both submissions. One agency per job.
  • Notice to quit. Most agreements require notice. Leaving without it can cost you unemployment eligibility or rehire status, and some carry a fee.
  • No-hire and non-solicit clauses. Common. They can block you from being hired by the client directly for up to a year. Ask how long.
  • Timecard deadlines. Miss the cutoff and your pay is held to the next cycle. Find out the day and time.
  • "Subject to change or cancellation." Start dates can slip. Don't quit another job until you have a confirmed start.
  • Rate in the RTR must match the offer. If the right-to-represent form says one number and the offer letter says another, stop and get it fixed before you sign.
  • Inventions and work product. Anything you build on the job belongs to the client. Matters if you run a side business.

Not legal advice. Paste the agreement into an AI tool and ask it to list every obligation on you, then read those lines yourself.
